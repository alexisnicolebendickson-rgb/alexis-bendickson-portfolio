The Productive Soul

On Charles Taylor, the moment ordinary work became holy — and why the productive self was a choice, not a destiny.

Alexis Bendickson · Course essay on Charles Taylor, Sources of the Self, Fall 2026

Taylor's question in Sources of the Self is how the modern self — the self that understands itself by looking within — came to exist at all. His answer comes as a historiography, and the word matters. A historiography is not just a history; it is a particular writing of one — which events get chosen, and how they are strung into a story a culture can recognise itself in. And this one is the history of a single tradition. Taylor is mapping the Western point of view, the line of philosophy and theology that runs from Plato forward, so that "the modern identity" in his pages is that tradition's identity, told from inside it. The answer he builds across Parts I and II runs in stages. Plato locates value outside the self, in an "order of things [that] embodies an ontic logos" (p. 186); Augustine turns the search inward, but only as a step on the way upward to God, since "the path inward was only a step on the way upward" (p. 156); Descartes makes the turn self-sufficient, aiming at "a quite self-sufficient certainty… which I can generate for myself by following the right method" (p. 156); Montaigne turns the inward gaze on the ordinary self as such; and Locke appears to complete the arc with the punctual self, for whom "we become constructors of our own character" (p. 197). Each stage is the same inward move, pointed somewhere new. But that is only half the story — and Part III, which tells the other half, changes what the first half meant. Part III turns the inward self outward: in what Taylor calls the affirmation of ordinary life, it is fused with a Reformation ethic that sanctifies labour, marriage, and the family — and Locke, who looked like an endpoint, turns out to be the hinge of that fusion, the figure in whom self-service in productive form is reconciled with beneficence toward others. Taylor's account of this fusion is the most compelling part of his story so far.

It is also, I will argue, where the story's selectivity is easiest to see: the productivity at its centre was a contingent development within one tradition, not a necessary destination of inwardness itself — and Taylor's own theory of narrative, which Part III brings to the surface, gives us the terms to say why that matters.

"Ordinary life" is, Taylor warns, "a term of art I introduce to designate those aspects of human life concerned with production and reproduction, that is, labour, the making of the things needed for life, and our life as sexual beings, including marriage and the family" (p. 211). The Reformation's move was to sanctify exactly this: the affirmation "displaces the locus of the good life from some special range of higher activities and places it within 'life' itself. The full human life is now defined in terms of labour and production, on one hand, and marriage and family life, on the other" (p. 213). What pleases God is no longer which activity one performs but how one performs it. Joseph Hall: "God loveth adverbs; and cares not how good, but how well" (p. 224). Perkins draws the conclusion with deliberate shock: between "washing of dishes, and preaching of the word of God… as touching to please God none at all" (p. 224) — there is no difference. Taylor states the reversal in his own words: "The highest life can no longer be defined by an exalted kind of activity; it all turns on the spirit in which one lives whatever one lives, even the most mundane existence" (p. 224). Notice what has quietly happened: nothing is morally neutral anymore. The price of making every task holy is that no task is ever off duty.

This inward-looking, God-directed spirit is then joined to the disengaged philosophy traced in Parts I–II. Chapter 14 opens by naming "the fusion of the ethic of ordinary life and the philosophy of disengaged freedom and rationality" — a fusion which "has ended up generating what is perhaps the dominant outlook of modern Western technological society" (p. 234). Part III is not a new topic. It is the inwardness of Parts I–II, given a world to work in.

Locke is where the fusion becomes a single outlook: the self who seemed to end the story turns out to be its hinge. Locke "was a crucial figure in this phase — and indeed, in this whole development"; his thought "was one of the earliest embodiments of the synthesis," and for all his disengaged freedom he "remained in some sense a believing Christian, and one deeply influenced by the Puritan affirmation of ordinary life" (p. 234). The influence is specific: "In making our preservation the central point of God's will for us, Locke is following the Protestant affirmation of ordinary life… God wants us to be productive, and this means that we should give ourselves energetically and intelligently to some useful task" (p. 238). What God calls us to, on Locke's picture as Taylor reconstructs it, is "to act strenuously, and also efficaciously, to meet our needs, but with an eye also to the common good" (p. 239). This is the point at which Lockean self-construction acquires its social payoff: "This is how we ought to live: to be industrious and rational in meeting our own needs and hence, through the improvements which result, help to meet the needs of others." Locke's ordered life thus "helped to prepare the way for the conception of the high Enlightenment for which Halévy coined the term 'harmony of interests'. There is a way of life which can conciliate self-service and beneficence. A key feature of it is that our service of self takes productive form" (p. 239). Here, stated in Taylor's own vocabulary, is the mutual benefit that Parts I–II had not yet supplied. The theology can fall away and the shape survives intact, because it is the shape we still live in: be productive, improve yourself, and your self-interest will be counted, almost automatically, as a public good. Beneficence, here, is not an act but a sum: no one performs it; it is what productive selves are supposed to total. And here is Taylor's verdict on the figure who carries it: Locke "is in fact a crucial hinge figure in the evolution of the ethic of ordinary life from its original theological formulation to the modern, 'bourgeois' naturalist one" (p. 239). Even the adverbs have moved: "'God loveth adverbs', as Joseph Hall put it. In Locke's new transposition of the ethic, the crucial adverbs are shifting. Where in the pure Reform variant, it was a matter of living worshipfully for God, now it is becoming a question of living rationally" (p. 242).

I find this account persuasive, and part of the persuasion is methodological. Taylor's question was never simply what happened; it was the interpretive question of what the appeal of each move was, since "in this middle ground lies all adequate historical explanation" (p. 204). Part III vindicates that method at the level of culture: the shift he traces "takes place over the whole culture," and of the philosophers he adds, "they did not originate it, much less bring it about unaided" (p. 285) — they articulated a change already under way in callings, households, and practices.

But the same pages expose the method's limit. None of this should surprise a reader who keeps the book's genre in view — a historiography of the Western tradition will find its evidence where that tradition lived and wrote — but it fixes the scope of the conclusion. The culture whose practices carry this story is a particular culture. On Taylor's own telling, the disciplined ethic of work — "continuous disciplined work, work which should be of benefit to people and hence ought to be efficacious" — "must be recognized as one of the formative influences of the work ethic of modern capitalist culture, at least in the Anglo-Saxon world" (p. 226), and the ethic it feeds is the modern "'bourgeois'" one (p. 239). A hierarchy was genuinely abolished here — dishwashing can please God as much as preaching — but the same page that abolishes it installs another: "labour in a calling was a spiritually serious business, something we should give ourselves to not just intermittently but earnestly and unremittingly. Puritans exhorted their hearers to shun idleness," for "an idle man's brain becometh quickly the shop of the devil" (p. 224). The saying is funny until it stops being funny: an unoccupied mind is not at rest, it is available — and something worse will take the shift. It also raises a question Taylor's story does not stop to ask. What is the standing of a life that cannot produce — the sick, the old, the simply exhausted? If dignity is indexed to efficacious labour, those lives become a problem the ethic cannot solve, because it has no adverb for them: there is no well in which to do nothing. Nothing in the inwardness traced so far required this destination. Augustine's inward turn pointed upward, to God; Montaigne's pointed nowhere in particular, at the self as a subject worth studying for its own sake. What would a modern identity look like that had taken Montaigne's road instead — inwardness as self-study rather than self-improvement? Productivity is a further destination, supplied by a specific time, place, and confession — selected, not entailed. Taylor, to be clear, never claims entailment: his question is what the appeal was, and my point survives his answer only in a narrowed form, which is this: the appeal he reconstructs is the appeal within one tradition, but it is narrated — through a single hinge figure, in a single ascending line — as the formation of "the modern identity" as such. The problem is the smoothness of the hinge: it lets a development that Taylor's own evidence locates "at least in the Anglo-Saxon world" pass as a stage in the formation of modern identity as such.

What hangs on this? More than the accuracy of one history. Because of what Taylor taught us in Part I and states outright in Part III: "we grasp our lives in a narrative," and "in order to have a sense of who we are, we have to have a notion of how we have become, and of where we are going" (p. 47). The modern condition is that this narrative can no longer be borrowed. Where a medieval self could take its story "ready-made from the canonical models and archetypes," the modern self "can only find an identity in self-narration. Life has to be lived as a story… The story has to be drawn from the particular events and circumstances of this life," its meaning "seen as something that unfolds through the events" (p. 289). Taylor is honest about the tension inside this: a life's shape can look like "simply the result of the happenings as they accumulate," or like "something already latent, which emerges through what comes to pass" — "But they are obviously both inescapable. We are made what we are by events; and as self-narrators, we live these through a meaning which the events come to manifest or illustrate" (p. 289). This mode of life-narration, he concludes, "is the quintessentially modern one, that which fits the experience of the disengaged, particular self" (p. 289) — so much so that it determines the form of the modern novel, whose "formal realism" treats a life as "a full and authentic report of human experience" (p. 289).

Sources of the Self is itself such a narration — the West narrating itself: a culture that can no longer take its story from archetypes, telling itself how it became what it is, drawing the story from particular events — Augustine's study, Perkins's pulpit, Locke's study again. That is not an accusation; it is Taylor's method performing his thesis, and it is why the book persuades. But a narration's coherence is achieved by selection — of events, of hinges, of destinations — and Taylor's is no exception. To say the productivity at the story's centre was selected rather than necessary is not to refute the story. I end up agreeing with Taylor about the self we have. My disagreement is narrower: with how inevitable his story makes that self sound. It is to read Taylor the way Taylor teaches us to read a self: as a narrator whose account can be examined, questioned, and — where the events left out would tell it differently — retold.
