Remembering Who Was Already Here

Liberia’s motto says the love of liberty brought “us” here. This essay asks who that leaves out — and what changes when a country starts remembering everyone.

Alexis Bendickson · Course essay, Fall 2026

Liberia's national motto is still "The Love of Liberty Brought Us Here." It reveals who Liberia's official memory was built for — and who it leaves out. The "us" are the free and formerly enslaved Black Americans who settled under the American Colonization Society from 1822 and declared independence on July 26, 1847. The Indigenous majority already there do not appear at all. One statement-giver to the Truth and Reconciliation Commission put it bluntly: the seal "implies that there was no one in the country before 1822" (Minnesota Advocates for Human Rights, 2009). In Liberia, memory is about who counts as a founder, a citizen, and a victim in the present.

For over a century, that story was a governing order. From 1847 to 1980, the Americo-Liberian minority dominated the state, Indigenous Liberians were denied meaningful participation, and commemoration reinforced the hierarchy. Matilda Newport Day, made a holiday in 1916, celebrated a settler woman said to have lit a cannon against Indigenous attackers in 1822 — a story historians question, since no early account mentions her. Celebrating it each December 1 celebrated settler survival over Indigenous people. After Samuel Doe's 1980 coup ended 133 years of Americo-Liberian rule, his government abolished it (Holsoe, 2007).

But replacing one official memory with another is not the same as telling the truth. Doe, the first Indigenous president, was killed in 1990, early in civil wars that lasted, with an interruption, from 1989 to 2003 and killed an estimated 250,000 people (Center for Justice and Accountability, cited in GNN Liberia, 2025). The wars grew partly from the exclusions the founding myth normalized, yet postwar Liberia largely chose not to prosecute them. That choice drew on traditions like the palava hut, where forgiveness and harmony could outweigh blame (The New Humanitarian, 2022). I do not dismiss that ethic — after that much violence, wanting to live together is understandable. The problem is who benefited from forgetting.

Liberia's Truth and Reconciliation Commission did serious work: it collected statements from 20,000 people and heard testimony from over 800 Liberians at home and in the diaspora (Human Rights Watch, 2019). Its 2009 report found all warring factions had committed serious abuses and recommended an Extraordinary Criminal Court, reparations, and barring some figures from office. Almost none happened for fifteen years. Scholar Aaron Weah notes the report generated little political will because people named as notorious perpetrators held influential government positions, and winning the presidency seemed to require courting them as gatekeepers to vote-rich counties (The New Humanitarian, 2022). Former warlord Prince Johnson — present in 1990 as Doe was tortured to death — became a senator and kingmaker until his death in 2024. That is memory losing to electoral math.

That is why the last two years feel different. In May 2024, President Joseph Boakai created the Office of the War and Economic Crimes Court to design the court the TRC proposed in 2009 (Just Security, 2026). In 2025, the national human rights commission, with UN support, dedicated memorials at massacre and mass-grave sites in several counties, work tied to the TRC's memorialization recommendation (GNN Liberia, 2025). Most strikingly, Boakai oversaw reburial services for both Tolbert, executed in Doe's coup, and Doe himself, then issued the government's first apology to war victims: "To every victim of our civil conflict, to every family broken, to every dream shattered, we say: 'We are sorry'" (Reuters, July 8, 2025). Holding Tolbert and Doe in the same mourning refuses the old settler-versus-Indigenous script. At the 2025 Independence Day ceremony, the national orator likewise urged a commission to propose a new, inclusive motto (GNN Liberia, 2025).

Liberia is now testing whether memorialization can lead to accountability, or substitute for it. An apology and a reburial are meaningful to families who waited decades to hear their loved ones mattered. But a war crimes court still does not exist, and prosecutions have yet to begin. If the court stalls, future Liberians may remember 2024–2025 as many now remember the TRC: the moment the country described justice beautifully and declined to do it. If it is built, Liberia could offer comparative politics something rare — a society that chose silence to survive a war, later choosing memory not for revenge, but to change who its founding story, and its courts, are finally for. That would begin with the motto. Liberia does not need to erase the settlers' love of liberty. It needs to admit liberty was never theirs alone to bring.
