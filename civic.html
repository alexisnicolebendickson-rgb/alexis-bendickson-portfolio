Why I support them

A blue wave, by the numbers

There's real momentum building in Alabama right now. Madison County's Democratic primary turnout nearly tripled between 2022 and 2026 — from around 12,000 votes to more than 35,000 — and the Democratic share of the primary electorate jumped from 19% to 53%. People are calling it a possible blue wave, and honestly, being part of that energy on the ground — training volunteers, mapping voter data, watching people show up who haven't shown up in years — has been one of the most exciting things I've been part of. These are the candidates I'm backing this cycle, and the one campaign I'm interning for directly.

Source: al.com — "Alabama saw huge surge in Democratic voting in 2026 primary"

Interning for this campaign

James Linderholm

Alabama House District 20 — Huntsville

I'm the Coordinator (Intern) on James's campaign, and I've seen up close how much care he puts into this race. I manage voter file data and mapping, help train volunteers, and dig into policy research — and every time I bring him something, he actually reads it and asks good questions back. He spent 33 years running his own gymnastics business here in Huntsville before going to law school, so he understands what it's like to make payroll and keep a small business alive in this state. That background shows up in his platform: expanding Medicaid, fixing infrastructure instead of pouring more money into prisons, and investing in public education. He's not running on slogans — he's running on the same practical, show-up-and-do-the-work ethic he built his business on, and I believe in it.

Beyond the ballot

What's watching us

Something else I pay close attention to, especially given how much of my research is about how surveillance and control have historically fallen hardest on Black communities: automated license plate readers, or ALPRs. Most are made by a company called Flock Safety — cameras that photograph every car that passes, logging location, time, and identifying details into a searchable, shareable database. No warrant required.

The more I've read into this, the more it looks like the same pattern I study in my plantation-era work, just running on modern infrastructure. Investigations this year found law enforcement agencies running Flock searches using racial slurs targeting Romani communities, agencies tracking protesters exercising their First Amendment rights, and one query in Texas that swept across more than 83,000 cameras nationwide while investigating a woman for a self-managed abortion.

I don't think this is really about public safety. It's surveillance capitalism operating with a badge attached — a private company selling government agencies a nationwide tracking network with almost no oversight, then treating backlash as anti-police. It's warrantless, it disproportionately touches Black and brown communities and immigrants, and it feeds directly into the same carceral system my research tries to hold accountable.
